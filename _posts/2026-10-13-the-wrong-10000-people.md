Work in content long enough and someone will ask you the question:

"So how many views did your best piece get?"

It's a reasonable thing to ask. Views are the one content metric everybody understands. They're also the one that tells you the least about whether your content is doing its job.

## Reach measures distance, not destination

A view tells you how far a piece travelled, but it doesn't tell you where it landed. Ten thousand views could be ten thousand potential buyers, but it could also be students, competitors, job seekers, other marketers studying your hook, and your mum. The dashboard counts them all the same. And even the right people can read your piece, nod along and scroll away without remembering who published it five seconds later. Attention and brand value are not the same thing. In that case, the piece got the attention, but your brand got none of the value.

That's fine if you sell something everyone buys. If you sell shampoo, almost any pair of eyes belongs to a possible customer. More reach, more sales, roughly.

Problem is most companies don't sell shampoo.

## The maths of a small market

Take a company selling compliance software to mid-sized European banks. The organizations that could ever buy from it number in the hundreds. The people inside them with a say in the decision: maybe 3,000, generously. That's the whole audience.

Now this company publishes a post that gets 50,000 views. Everyone celebrates, and someone makes a slide.

A viral moment may feel like winning, but let’s do the maths: if the market size is 3,000 people, that means at least 47,000 of those views came from people who can't buy, won't buy, and don't know anyone who will.

Meanwhile, a dry, specific piece about a regulation change gets 400 views. If 150 of them come from those 3,000 people, it just reached 5% of the entire market in one go.

## So what does "best" look like?

In niche B2B, the best piece tends to be boring on a dashboard and busy everywhere else. You recognize it by what happens after you publish:

- A prospect mentions it on a sales call, unprompted.
- Sales starts sending it before meetings without being asked.
- It gets forwarded inside a target account.
- Someone with the right job title replies.

This is why reach alone is such a poor measure of whether content worked. The more useful questions come afterwards. Did more people search for the brand? Did the people who found you through the piece read anything else? Did the brand become associated with an idea it actually wants to own?

None of this shows up as a big number. Most of it doesn't show up at all unless you go looking: ask sales what came up on the call, and listen to what customers say brought them in.

## In fairness to reach

There's a well-known idea in B2B marketing called the 95-5 rule, from Professor John Dawes. At any given moment, roughly 95% of your potential buyers aren't in the market. They'll buy in a year, or in three. Being known by them before they need you matters a lot.

But notice whose attention that argument is about. Still the 3,000. Being famous among people who will actually buy your product.

So when someone asks how many views my best piece got, the honest answer is: just the right ones.
