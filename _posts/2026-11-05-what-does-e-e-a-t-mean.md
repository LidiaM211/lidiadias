## TL;DR

- E-⁠E-⁠A-⁠T stands for Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness.
- This is the framework Google gives its human quality raters to judge whether a page deserves to be believed. It isn’t a ranking factor you can switch on, but it describes what Google’s systems are trying to reward.
- Since January 2025, the raters’ manual gives low ratings to low-effort AI-generated pages, making first-hand experience the hardest letter to fake.
- Google’s guidance describes what it’s aiming for, not what it guarantees. The safer bet is content nobody else could have written, because that works on human readers, whatever the algorithms do.

Sooner or later, you’ll hear it: “We need to work on our EEAT.”

The internet has never had this much content. Since early 2025, roughly half of new English-language articles have been mostly AI-generated, according to an [analysis of 65,000 web pages](https://graphite.io/five-percent/more-articles-are-now-created-by-ai-than-humans) by the SEO firm Graphite. The same research found something more interesting: those articles rarely show up in Google results or ChatGPT answers, because volume isn’t visibility. E-⁠E-⁠A-⁠T is Google’s answer. It describes the quality bar that decides two things at once: whether the machine quotes you, and whether the person keeps reading.

## Where the letters come from and what each letter actually asks for

Google pays thousands of people to search for things and rate the results. Their ratings don’t move rankings directly. Google uses them to check whether its algorithms are doing what it wants. To keep those people consistent, it gives them a long manual: the Search Quality Rater Guidelines. For years, that manual asked raters to look at E-A-T. In December 2022, Google added a second E, for Experience, and the acronym got the awkward double letter it has today. E-⁠E-⁠A-⁠T, then, is a description written for humans of what a page worth trusting looks like.

Each letter asks for a specific kind of evidence:

- **Experience.** Has the person writing this actually done the thing? Like an article about switching CRM systems by someone who lived through the migration.
- **Expertise.** Does the person know the subject well? A tax guide written by a certified accountant; a medical page reviewed by a doctor.
- **Authoritativeness.** Is this a source people go to for this topic? You can’t award this one to yourself. It depends on who cites you and who invites you to speak.
- **Trust.** Is the page accurate and honest? You earn it by getting the facts right, showing where they come from, making clear who is behind the page, and correcting mistakes when you make them. Google is unusually direct here. In its own documentation: “Of these aspects, trust is most important.”

## One bar, two readers

Your content now has to clear the same bar for two readers: the machine that decides whether to quote you, and the person who decides whether to keep reading. They’re looking for the same thing, for different reasons.

Two things to know before going further. First, Google says plainly that “E-⁠E-⁠A-⁠T itself isn’t a specific ranking factor.” There’s no score. It describes what Google’s systems aim at, not which buttons to press. Second, the bar rises on “Your Money or Your Life” topics: health, finances, safety, well-being. If your B2B blog gives finance, legal, or health guidance, even in a friendly post, it’s read against the stricter standard.

## Reader one: the machine

When someone asks Google a question today, the first thing they often see is an AI Overview, and far fewer people click past it. What counts now is being one of the few sources that summary is built on.

Google says there’s no special trick to getting in, just “helpful, reliable, people-first content.” Since January 2025, its raters’ manual gives the Lowest rating to pages that are mostly auto- or AI-generated with little effort, originality, or added value.

This is what Google is aiming for, not a promise of what it does. A model can’t check whether you really lived through that CRM migration you’re talking about. It sees proxies: who links to you, whether you already rank, whether others mention you. So experience alone won’t get you quoted. What it does is give the machine something worth quoting once you’re in the running, something the other forty-nine explainers don’t have. The same logic applies to ChatGPT and Perplexity: they also need a reason to pick you over everyone saying the same thing.

## Reader two: the human

Fewer people click through from search now. In a [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) study of real browsing data, people who saw an AI summary clicked a result in 8% of visits, compared with 15% for people who didn’t see one. Google argues the clicks that remain are “higher quality.” Maybe, but that’s Google grading its own product. The safer reading is that the people who still click have already read the summary. They don’t need the basics again, they came for something the summary couldn’t give them.

That’s where Experience earns its letter. A model can sound like an expert because it has read what experts write. It can’t have lived through the CRM migration.

I used to sit close to the sales team, close enough to hear the same question come up again and again. Every explainer about an upcoming regulation covered what the law required in general. What prospects actually wanted to know was what it required from them, specifically. None of the top-ranking articles answered that, so we wrote the piece that did, and it became one of the biggest sources of traffic to our website. That question never showed up in keyword tools, it showed up in sales calls.

The bottom line is that a longer version of the overview won’t keep them; something only the writer could know will.

## How small companies can use E-⁠E-⁠A-⁠T to compete

Most companies already have the raw material E-⁠E-⁠A-⁠T asks for. The people who built the product know why it works the way it does, and the people who talk to customers know which problems keep coming back.

For a small company, the hard part is everything around it. There’s rarely a content team, sometimes not even one marketer. The founder is usually the person with the most experience and the least time, so content becomes the thing that gets done on Friday afternoon, or handed to a tool, and ends up as an eleventh version of the top ten results. That’s exactly the kind of page an AI Overview can summarize without sending anyone your way.

Startups are often closer to the problem than anyone else writing about it, so Experience is rarely what they lack. Authority is. A new domain with no links and no mentions can publish the best post on a topic and still sit on page three. That’s the letter you can’t award yourself, but you can make it easier for others to award it to you:

- **Publish one thing others will cite.** A small piece of original data, like a survey of 50 customers or a benchmark from your own product, travels further than ten opinion posts.
- **Borrow other people’s audiences.** Find your way through podcasts, guest newsletters, and/or panels in your niche: each one is a mention, often a link, and a sign that someone else vouches for you.
- **Write with your customers.** A co-authored post or a named case study brings their credibility to your page.
- **Don’t bet everything on search.** Send the same post to your newsletter subscribers, your sales team, your LinkedIn network. If it’s good enough for E-⁠E-⁠A-⁠T, it’s good enough to help close a deal, whether or not Google ever notices.

So what does E-⁠E-⁠A-⁠T mean for small companies? It isn’t a checklist for pleasing Google. It’s a description of content nobody else could have written, which works on the buyer today and is what the machines are trying to get better at finding. It’s the bar your content has to clear twice: once for the machine that quotes it, and once for the buyer who clicks.
